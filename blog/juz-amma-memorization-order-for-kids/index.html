Juz Amma Memorization Order for Kids: Which Surahs to Learn First

A practical order for children to memorise Juz Amma, from Al-Fatiha and the shortest surahs to An-Naba, with verse counts, four stages and a revision plan.

Many families begin Quran memorisation with Juz Amma, the thirtieth and final part of the Quran. Its surahs are short, they are often recited in prayer, and children hear many of them at home and in the mosque.

Parents then ask a practical question: in what order should my child memorise these surahs?

This guide sets out a common teaching order in four stages, listed in the order a child would usually learn them, with the number of verses in each surah, and explains why a teacher may sometimes change that order for a particular child.

Why children usually start at the end of the Mushaf

Juz Amma contains 37 surahs, from Surah An-Naba (78) to Surah An-Nas (114). The surahs near the end of the Mushaf are generally the shortest, so many teachers start with An-Nas and work backwards towards An-Naba.

This is a practical teaching order, not a religious requirement. Its benefit is simple: the child begins with surahs they can complete quickly, which builds confidence before longer surahs are introduced.

Most children also learn Surah Al-Fatiha first. It is not part of Juz Amma, but it is recited in every unit of prayer, so it is usually the first surah a child memorises.

These surahs have between three and seven verses. Many children already know parts of them from hearing them at home.

The number of verses is not the same as difficulty

Counting verses is a useful guide, but it does not tell the whole story.

  • Al-Bayyinah has only 8 verses, but they are long. Some teachers leave it until after Al-Qadr, At-Tin or Ash-Sharh.
  • Al-Asr has 3 short verses and is sometimes taught earlier than its position suggests.
  • Surahs with similar openings or repeated phrases, such as Al-Falaq and An-Nas, may be easier to keep apart when they are revised together.
  • A child who is still learning to read Arabic may find a surah with unfamiliar sounds harder than a longer surah they already hear often.

For this reason, treat the order above as a starting point. Your child’s teacher may move a surah earlier or later after listening to your child recite.

How long does Juz Amma take?

There is no fixed timeline. The pace depends on the child’s age, reading level, practice routine and how much revision older surahs need.

A younger child who learns mainly by listening may move more slowly through the longer surahs. An older child who reads well may learn new verses faster but still needs regular review to keep them.

It is more useful to ask, “Is my child still reciting earlier surahs well?” than “How many surahs has my child finished?”

Keep a simple revision cycle

New memorisation is only part of the work. Without revision, surahs learned a few months earlier can fade.

A simple three-part revision cycle

Part What to practise How often
NewThe current short section of the surah being learnedMost practice days
RecentThe last few surahs your child completedSeveral times a week
OlderEarlier surahs, rotated so each is heard regularlyOne or two each week

When a recent surah becomes weak, pause new memorisation for a day or two and strengthen it. This is part of memorisation, not a delay.

Use a simple progress checklist

A short checklist can make progress visible without turning practice into an exam. For each surah, you might note one of three states:

  • Learning — the child is still working through its sections.
  • Memorised — the child can recite it from memory with little help.
  • Needs review — the child knew it before but now hesitates or mixes verses.

Children often enjoy colouring in or ticking a surah when it moves to “memorised”. Keep the focus on steady effort rather than speed.

For more ideas on recording progress, see how parents can track their child’s Quran progress.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Moving to the next surah before the current one is secure.
  • Stopping revision of earlier surahs once Juz Amma feels “mostly done”.
  • Practising only with audio, so pronunciation mistakes are never corrected.
  • Switching between several reciters for the same surah.
  • Comparing your child’s pace with siblings or classmates.

Start small and keep going

Begin with Al-Fatiha and the shortest surahs, move through the stages at a pace your child can manage, and keep earlier surahs active as you go. A steady routine and clear correction matter more than reaching An-Naba quickly.
